The quiet return of the analog interface

After a decade of flat, frictionless screens, designers are rediscovering knobs, detents, and the reassuring weight of a control that pushes back.

MW
Mara Whitfield 14 min read · July 21, 2026
Above: a hardware prototype from an independent research lab, photographed under raking light.

There is a particular sound a well-made rotary encoder makes — a soft, mechanical stitch of clicks as it turns — that no touchscreen has ever managed to reproduce. For years we treated that sound as a relic. Now it is becoming a feature request.

The shift did not happen all at once. It started at the edges: a boutique synthesizer here, a camera with a real dial there. Users who had grown fluent in glass began to notice what glass could not give them — the ability to adjust without looking, to feel where a value sat, to trust their hands.

A good control tells you where you are before you've read a single number.

Weight as information

Interface designers borrow a lot of language from the physical world — depth, elevation, friction — but for most of the last decade those were metaphors rendered in shadow and blur. What is different now is the appetite to make them literal again, to let resistance carry meaning.

Consider the humble slider. On a screen it is a line and a dot. Given a detent — a subtle notch at the halfway mark — it becomes a decision. You feel the center. You can return to it without aiming. The information lives in the motion, not the label.

Designing for the hand

This is not nostalgia, or not only. It is a recognition that attention is finite, and that offloading some of it to muscle memory frees the eyes for the work that actually matters. The best new tools are quietly bilingual: fluent in pixels, but unafraid of texture.

Whether this becomes a lasting movement or a passing texture depends on whether the people building software can resist the urge to flatten everything back down. So far, the knobs are winning.
